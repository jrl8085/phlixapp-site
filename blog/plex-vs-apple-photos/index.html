Plex Photos vs Apple Photos — Managing Your Photo Library

By Jason Loomis · September 1, 2026 · Updated November 27, 2026 · 10 min read

If you have thousands (or hundreds of thousands) of photos, you have two fundamentally different approaches to managing them: let Apple handle everything in the cloud with Apple Photos, or keep full control with Plex Media Server on your own hardware. Both work. Both have serious trade-offs. And as someone who has run both systems simultaneously, I can tell you that neither is complete on its own -- but the gap between them is shrinking, especially with apps like Phlix, an independent photo app that works with Plex Media Server.

Apple Photos: Strengths

Apple Photos is the gold standard for consumer photo management, and it is hard to overstate how much Apple has invested in making it effortless:

  • iCloud sync: Every photo you take is automatically uploaded and available on every Apple device. No setup, no configuration, no thinking about it.
  • Search: On-device machine learning identifies people, places, objects, scenes, and even text in photos. Search for "dog at beach" and it works. Search for "receipts" and it finds them.
  • Memories: Apple automatically creates curated slideshows and collages from your library, complete with music.
  • Editing: Non-destructive editing tools built into the app. Crop, adjust, apply filters, and remove backgrounds without leaving the library.
  • Live Photos and video: Seamless handling of Live Photos, Cinematic mode video, and spatial video.
  • Sharing: Shared Albums and Shared Libraries make it easy to collaborate with family members.
  • Performance: The native Photos app handles libraries of any size with smooth scrolling, instant search, and optimized storage that keeps thumbnails on-device while full-resolution originals stay in the cloud.

Apple Photos: Weaknesses

  • iCloud storage costs: The free tier is 5 GB, which is laughable for photo storage. A family with 200,000+ photos will need the 2 TB plan at $9.99/month ($120/year) or more. Over five years, that is $600+ in recurring costs with no end in sight.
  • Vendor lock-in: Your entire photo library is tied to the Apple ecosystem. Exporting is possible but cumbersome, and you lose metadata, albums, and Memories in the process.
  • Privacy: While Apple encrypts iCloud data, your photos are still stored on someone else's servers. For privacy-conscious users, this is a non-starter.
  • No self-hosting option: You cannot run Apple Photos on your own hardware. Apple controls the infrastructure, the pricing, and the terms of service.

Plex Photos: Strengths

  • Self-hosted: Your photos live on your own hardware. A NAS, a desktop, or even a Raspberry Pi. No monthly fees, no storage limits, no third-party access.
  • No subscription costs: Once you have the hardware, photo storage and serving costs nothing. A 4 TB hard drive at $80 holds roughly 800,000 high-resolution photos. Compare that to $120/year for 2 TB of iCloud storage.
  • Full control: You control the file structure, backups, and access. Photos are standard files on a filesystem, not locked inside a proprietary database.
  • EXIF metadata: Plex reads EXIF data (date, camera model, GPS coordinates, focal length) and uses it for organization and display.
  • Sharing via Plex: Share your photo library with family and friends through Plex's user sharing system, just like you would share a movie library.
  • Integration with your media library: Photos sit alongside your movies, TV shows, and music in a single platform.

Plex Photos: Trade-offs

Here is where honesty matters. Plex's photo features differ from Apple Photos in important ways:

  • Search: Apple Photos recognizes people, places, objects, and text in your photos. In Plex you find photos mostly by their folders, dates, and other metadata, so a tidy folder structure and correct EXIF dates matter more.
  • Editing happens elsewhere: Plex is for organizing and viewing your photos; you crop, adjust, and edit them in other apps.
  • No camera upload: Plex announced on its forum on March 18, 2021 that it was ending Camera Upload, with removal targeted for June 2021, so getting new phone photos onto your server is a separate step (see "Use both" below).

On iPhone and iPad, Plex Inc. makes a separate app for photo libraries, Plex Photos. Its App Store page lists Recommended View, Timeline View and Library View as its supported features.

How Phlix Bridges the Gap

This is where Phlix comes in. Phlix is an independent iPhone and iPad app that works with Plex Media Server, built for browsing and showing your Plex photos.

Chronological Timeline with Year Scrubber

Phlix presents your entire Plex photo library as a chronological timeline, just like the native Photos app, one tap away from the folders on your server. A year scrubber on the side lets you tap a year to see just that year.

Big-Screen Slideshows on Your TV

With Screen Mirroring on, or over an HDMI adapter, the TV shows just the photos while your iPhone shows the controls. This is a Phlix Pro feature; without Pro, the TV mirrors your iPhone's screen. Phlix asks your server for an image sized to the display, with hard cuts between photos. It turns your Plex photo library into a digital picture frame on your largest screen.

Save to Photos

Save photos from your Plex server to your Photos app, one at a time. No Plex Pass is needed on your home network. The free tier includes 25 saves, and Phlix Pro unlocks unlimited saves.

Camera Upload (Beta)

Plex's own apps no longer upload your camera roll. Camera Upload (Beta) in Phlix does: on your home Wi-Fi, it backs up the photos and videos on your iPhone or iPad to a folder of your Plex photo library, through a free helper you run in Docker next to Plex Media Server. The first 100 uploads are free, and Phlix Pro uploads the rest (Camera Upload setup guide).

Performance with Large Libraries

Phlix is built for big libraries: it loads a page at a time instead of everything at once, and on cellular or in Low Data Mode it stops loading ahead of you.

When to Use Each

Choose Apple Photos if:

  • You want zero setup and zero maintenance
  • Smart search (people, places, objects) is important to you
  • You edit photos frequently on your phone
  • You are deeply invested in the Apple ecosystem and do not mind iCloud costs
  • You have fewer than 50,000 photos

Choose Plex (with Phlix) if:

  • You want to own your photos on your own hardware
  • You have a large library (50,000+ photos) and want to avoid cloud storage costs
  • Privacy and data sovereignty matter to you
  • You already run a Plex server for movies and TV
  • You want to display photos on your TV as a slideshow
  • You share photos with family through Plex

Use both if:

There is nothing stopping you from using Apple Photos for day-to-day capture and editing while maintaining a Plex library as your long-term archive. Copy new photos from your camera roll to your NAS now and then (with a transfer app such as PhotoSync, or by hand through the Files app), add them to your Plex library, and browse them with Phlix. Or let Phlix do the copying: Camera Upload (Beta) in Phlix copies the photos and videos on your iPhone or iPad into a folder of your Plex photo library on your home Wi-Fi, using a free helper you run in Docker next to Plex Media Server (Camera Upload setup guide). It needs Phlix 1.0.21 or later. You get the best of both worlds: Apple's polish for everyday use and self-hosted permanence for your archive.

The Bottom Line

Apple Photos is a better photo management tool in almost every way -- if you are willing to pay for iCloud storage and trust Apple with your data. Plex is a better photo storage and ownership strategy -- if you are willing to manage your own hardware and accept fewer features.

With Phlix, the gap between the two narrows: Camera Upload (Beta) backs up your iPhone photos to your server at home, and you get a date timeline with a year scrubber, big-screen slideshows, and saves to the Photos app on iPhone and iPad. It does not replace Apple's smart search or editing tools, but it gives your self-hosted library a photo app that feels at home on iOS.
