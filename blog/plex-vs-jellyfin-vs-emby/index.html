Plex vs Jellyfin vs Emby — Which Media Server Is Best in 2026?

By Jason Loomis · September 1, 2026 · 10 min read

Choosing a media server in 2026 means weighing three serious contenders: Plex, the polished commercial veteran; Jellyfin, the fully open-source upstart; and Emby, the middle-ground option that sits somewhere between the two. Each one can organize your movies, TV shows, music, and photos on your own hardware and stream them to nearly any device in your house or across the internet.

But they are not interchangeable. After running all three on identical hardware for over a year, here is an honest breakdown of where each one excels, where each one falls short, and which one deserves your time depending on what you actually care about.

Pricing Breakdown

Plex

Plex's free tier is functional but deliberately limited. You can organize your library, stream locally and remotely, and use the web player at no cost. But features most power users want -- hardware transcoding, mobile downloads, Skip Intro, Live TV/DVR, and lyrics -- require Plex Pass. It costs $6.99 per month, $69.99 per year, or a one-time $749 for lifetime access.

That lifetime price has climbed dramatically over the years. In 2012 it was $75. In 2024 it hit $249. As of early 2026 it sits at $749. For many users who bought in early, it remains the single best deal in self-hosted software. For newcomers, the calculus is very different.

Plex also introduced the Remote Watch Pass in 2026, which lets shared users pay for their own remote streaming access rather than requiring the server owner to hold a Plex Pass.

Jellyfin

Jellyfin is free. Completely, permanently, no-asterisks free. There is no paid tier, no premium unlock, no subscription. Hardware transcoding, Live TV, mobile sync -- everything is included. The project is funded by donations and maintained by volunteers. If your primary criterion is cost, Jellyfin wins by default.

Emby

Emby mirrors Plex's freemium model but at a lower price point. Emby Premiere costs $119 for a lifetime license, which unlocks hardware transcoding, Live TV/DVR, mobile sync, and parental controls. Compared to Plex's $749 lifetime, Emby's pricing feels reasonable -- but it is still a closed-source product competing with a free alternative.

Client App Support

Plex has the widest client ecosystem by a significant margin. Official apps exist for iOS, Android, Apple TV, Fire TV, Roku, PlayStation, Xbox, smart TVs from Samsung, LG, and Vizio, and virtually every web browser. The apps are polished, regularly updated, and consistent across platforms.

Jellyfin's client story is improving but uneven. The Android and Android TV apps are solid. The iOS app is functional but less refined than Plex's. There is no native Roku app -- a significant gap for many households. The web client works well but relies on browser codec support for Direct Play. Third-party clients like Swiftfin (iOS) and Infuse help fill the gaps.

Emby falls between the two. Official apps cover most major platforms including Roku, and the experience is generally polished, if not quite as refined as Plex.

Transcoding and Performance

All three servers use FFmpeg under the hood for transcoding. Performance differences come down to optimization, hardware acceleration support, and how aggressively each server avoids transcoding in the first place.

Plex handles hardware transcoding (Intel Quick Sync, NVIDIA NVENC, AMD VCE) well, but locks it behind Plex Pass. Its tone mapping for HDR-to-SDR conversion is among the best.

Jellyfin offers hardware transcoding for free and has made substantial improvements to HDR tone mapping in recent releases. For users with Intel Quick Sync hardware, it is highly capable. Configuration requires more manual work than Plex, especially in Docker environments.

Emby handles hardware transcoding competently with Premiere. Its implementation is mature and well-documented.

User Interface and Polish

This is where Plex pulls ahead most visibly. Plex's UI is cohesive, fast, and attractive across every platform. It feels like a commercial product because it is one. The dashboard, library views, and player controls are intuitive even for non-technical family members.

Jellyfin's interface has improved significantly since its early days but still shows its volunteer-driven development. Custom CSS themes help, but the out-of-the-box experience is functional rather than beautiful. The admin dashboard, however, is excellent and more transparent than Plex's.

Emby's UI lands in between. It is more customizable than Plex but less polished. The media detail pages and player work well, but the overall aesthetic feels dated compared to Plex.

Photo Support

This is an area where all three servers are weak -- but Plex has the most developed photo features.

Plex supports photo libraries with EXIF metadata, basic albums, and a timeline view in its web and mobile apps. It reads camera data, handles RAW thumbnails, and displays photos adequately for casual browsing. However, the experience with large libraries (50,000+ photos) is slow, and the iOS photo browsing experience lacks a proper year scrubber, fast scrolling, or AirPlay slideshow support.

That gap is exactly what Phlix was built to fill. Phlix connects to your Plex server and provides a chronological timeline, year scrubber, 4K AirPlay slideshows, and offline downloads -- the features Plex's own app does not offer for photos.

Jellyfin has minimal photo support. It can display images in a library but offers no timeline, no EXIF-based organization, and no slideshow features worth mentioning.

Emby offers basic photo browsing similar to Plex but with fewer features and less community tooling around it.

Community and Ecosystem

Plex has the largest third-party ecosystem: Tautulli for monitoring, Overseerr for media requests, Phlix for photo browsing, Prologue for audiobooks, Plexamp for music, and hundreds of scripts and tools. The community is massive and well-resourced.

Jellyfin's community is passionate and growing quickly. Being open-source, it attracts developers who contribute plugins, themes, and integrations. The plugin ecosystem is smaller than Plex's but more flexible.

Emby's community is the smallest of the three. It has a plugin system and an active forum, but the ecosystem is noticeably thinner.

The Verdict

Choose Plex if you want the most polished experience, the widest client support, and you are willing to pay for Plex Pass (or can live without its locked features). It is the best option for households where non-technical users need to navigate the interface. It is also the only option with strong photo support, especially when paired with Phlix.

Choose Jellyfin if you prioritize open-source software, want every feature for free, and do not mind doing more configuration work. It is the ethical choice and the budget choice. Just be prepared for a rougher client experience on some platforms.

Choose Emby if you want something between the two -- more polished than Jellyfin, cheaper than Plex, but without the ecosystem depth of either. It is a solid product that often gets overlooked.

For most users in 2026, the real decision is between Plex and Jellyfin. If you have already invested in Plex Pass (especially at the old lifetime price), there is little reason to switch. If you are starting fresh and budget matters, Jellyfin deserves serious consideration.
